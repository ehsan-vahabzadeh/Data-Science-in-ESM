# Bias adjustment for weather forecasts

Research checked 10 November 2026. This note concerns post-processing a numerical weather prediction (NWP) forecast after it is produced, using earlier matched forecast/reference pairs. It does not change the equations inside IFS or assimilate new observations into it. ERA5 is the project's initial reference, not direct ground truth.

## What we tested and what operational methods do

The two-day demonstration in [data-access history](data-access-history.md#two-day-bias-adjustment-demonstration) used a legitimate **additive mean bias**: the mean of `reference − forecast` over training samples was added to the next forecast. Additive and multiplicative mean scaling are explicitly used as weather forecast bias-correction methods in a [wind power forecast study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.seta.2025.104599). But one offset from one day for all locations and lead times is a minimal demonstration, not a representative operational implementation or evidence that bias correction cannot help. It used a reference unavailable at the next forecast's initialization.

Operational NWP post-processing often updates a bias using past verified forecast errors, with estimates specific to grid point or station and forecast lead. Some methods weight recent errors more heavily. NOAA describes an **exponentially decaying average** used for its ensemble forecasts: with `e = reference − forecast`, update `b_new = (1 − α) b_old + α e`, then add `b_new` to future forecasts. The cited study considered a range of weights; the right weight depends on data and verification, so its reported 0.02 is an example, not a universal parameter. [NOAA ensemble bias-correction study](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/wefo/27/2/waf-d-11-00011_1.xml). A wind forecasting study applied a Kalman filter to track evolving wind forecast bias at a wind farm; its reported gains are site- and dataset-specific, not guaranteed here. [Kalman-filter wind study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41601-021-00214-x).

## Candidate baselines before a multivariable ML model

1. **Raw TIGGE forecast:** essential reference; score the same matched cases as every adjustment.
2. **Training-period additive mean:** one fixed offset per variable. A transparent starting baseline. Fit on a substantial earlier period, not a single day; choose grouping and windows on validation data.
3. **Location/lead-dependent bias:** estimate separate historical mean offsets by grid cell (or pooled nearby cells / land-sea region) and lead time; consider seasonal or time-of-day groups where enough training data exist. More groups need more samples; sparse groups should fall back to a pooled estimate.
4. **Rolling or exponentially weighted bias:** calculate estimates from a trailing window of previously verified forecast errors, optionally by lead and location; recent data get more influence in the exponentially weighted version. Fix the initial estimate, minimum sample count, update ordering, missing-data rules and any reference-publication delay before evaluation. NOAA's example concerns different models and fields, so verify transferability. [NOAA](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/wefo/27/2/waf-d-11-00011_1.xml).
5. **Multiplicative scaling:** multiply the forecast by an estimated reference-to-forecast mean ratio. This can suit nonnegative quantities, but is unstable when the denominator is small and can distort extremes; use documented thresholds and evaluate against an additive baseline. [Wind power forecast study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.seta.2025.104599).
6. **Quantile mapping:** learn a transformation aligning the forecast and reference distributions, potentially correcting different parts of the distribution differently. It requires enough training data and careful treatment of extremes and zero values; improvement in distribution does not guarantee better errors at matching times or correct spatial dependence. A study of wind-resource estimation found mapping helpful in its own context; it does not guarantee day-ahead forecast improvement here. [Wind resource study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960148125005890); [limitations for extreme quantiles](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/17/8173/2024/). Climate-model distribution adjustment is related but should not be assumed equivalent to correcting paired weather forecasts. [Climate bias-adjustment comparison](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/17/1249/2024/).

## Wind, solar and operational timing

- **Wind:** start with matched 10 m wind speed; keep it nonnegative and report MAE, RMSE and signed bias, including by lead, season and land/sea. Compare corrections to u/v components versus speed only as a distinct experiment: vector and scalar adjustment are not equivalent. The current project does not have 100 m TIGGE wind.
- **Solar:** the current TIGGE field is accumulated **net** shortwave radiation, not incoming downward irradiance. Convert forecast and ERA5 to matching six-hour interval means before fitting. Day/night and solar angle matter; a constant positive correction during darkness is physically inappropriate. A published solar-forecast study investigates condition-dependent biases using solar zenith angle and a clear-sky index, but its results concern irradiance and locations that differ from this dataset. [Solar NWP study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0038092X11000570).
- **Data availability:** ERA5T is usually about five days behind real time, while final ERA5 arrives later and ERA5T may be revised. A simulated rolling correction may update only when the chosen reference is actually available, with its version recorded. A retrospective study using final ERA5 without publication-lag simulation should be labelled accordingly. [ECMWF ERA5T announcement](https://www.ecmwf.int/en/about/media-centre/news/2026/era5t-reanalysis-data); [ERA5 data documentation](https://confluence.ecmwf.int/plugins/viewsource/viewpagesrc.action?pageId=540945091). TIGGE's 48-hour archive release is itself unsuitable for a live day-ahead feed without another timely source. [TIGGE licence](https://ecds.ecmwf.int/licences/tigge-licence).

## Suggested project experiment

First collect and audit enough matched ERA5 coverage for the already-downloaded TIGGE period, including timestamps, the UK land/maritime mask, grid coordinates and solar accumulation intervals. Establish chronological training, validation and final test periods with all grid points from a day in the same partition. Fix the assumed ERA5T/final reference availability and forecast availability; never update using future test observations. Compare raw forecasts, a global mean offset, then location/lead or land-sea/lead rolling and exponentially weighted offsets. Tune grouping and window/decay using validation only; evaluate the untouched final period once. Report raw and adjusted bias, MAE and RMSE on the same rows, by lead, season and land/sea, with daylight solar treated separately. Use percentage errors only with a disclosed low-reference threshold and report coverage. A correction that reduces bias can worsen MAE/RMSE, so compare all of them. Independent station observations can later establish whether improvement against ERA5 translates to measured weather.
