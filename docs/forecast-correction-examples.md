# Examples of weather forecast correction and evaluation

**Research checked:** 6 November 2026. These examples inform the proposed UK experiment in [forecast-reference matching](forecast-reference-matching.md); none establishes that correction improves this project's forecasts. The papers and code address different targets, lead times and references, so their results are not interchangeable.

## Forecast post-processing examples

**EUPPBench: IFS forecasts paired with ERA5 or stations.** [Demaeyer et al. (2023)](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/15/2635/2023/) introduce an analysis-ready benchmark with ECMWF deterministic and ensemble forecasts, reforecasts, gridded ERA5 and station data. Its initial published comparison is specifically **2 m temperature**, not wind or solar: it post-processes ensemble forecasts at the nearest forecast grid point to each station. The [dataset documentation](https://eupp-benchmark.github.io/EUPPBench-doc/files/EUPPBench_datasets.html) describes the gridded subset and its ERA5 pairing; the [official access repository](https://github.com/EUPP-benchmark/climetlab-eumetnet-postprocessing-benchmark) provides a data plugin. The benchmark contains wind fields, including 100 m components, but the paper's temperature result is not evidence of 100 m wind correction skill. The small gridded benchmark covers central Europe, 45.75–53.5° N and 2.5–10.5° E; it does not cover the UK mainland, though some UK offshore areas could overlap. Check the domain, variables, station access and license before planning a UK download.

The benchmark distinguishes daily operational forecasts from reforecasts: the latter recompute historical forecasts with the model version current at the reforecast date. Its design provides matched valid times and locations, but an operational replay still requires checking when each forecast and training reference was actually available. The broad base dataset has time-granular access and cannot be sliced by issuance time according to its [access documentation](https://eupp-benchmark.github.io/EUPPBench-doc/files/base_datasets.html). [EUMETNET](https://www.eumetnet.eu/forecasting-and-climate/post-processing/) describes 2017–18 training and 2019 test data, 00 UTC daily forecasts and twice-weekly reforecasts. Do not pool them as though every reforecast were issued in its historical target year.

**NOAA ML4BC: another physical forecast model corrected against ERA5.** NOAA's [ML4BC repository](https://github.com/NOAA-EMC/ML4BC) and [technical paper](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/67181/noaa_67181_DS1.pdf) document a concrete collection pipeline: GFS forecasts from NOAA's AWS archive, ERA5 through the Copernicus Climate Data Store, and surface observations from the NCAR archive. The project pairs forecast valid times to ERA5 on a 0.25° grid. The published experiment targets US **2 m temperature**, training on 2021–2023 and testing in 2024; it also checks results against station observations. It demonstrates that using a different physical forecast model from the reference is a standard option, but its result does not establish UK wind or solar skill. The paper uses a neural network; the same paired-data principle supports a simpler linear baseline.

**Marine wind with independent observations.** The authors' [marine wind repository](https://github.com/Earth-Intelligence-Lab/marine-wind-forecasting), [preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.03606), and [processed dataset](https://zenodo.org/records/17793234) describe correction of GFS 10 m wind using previous observations and validation against ICOADS marine observations, with ERA5 also present in the data. This is an Atlantic marine-wind study and uses a transformer; it is not an IFS/ERA5 100 m or UK solar example. Its separate, already paired train/validation/test datasets demonstrate another collection approach, but the files are roughly 4 GB per lead time and their own terms and assumptions must be checked.

**Wind-speed ensemble calibration.** [Thorarinsdottir and Gneiting](https://stat.uw.edu/research/tech-reports/probabilistic-forecasts-wind-speed-ensemble-model-output-statistics-using-heteroskedastic-censored) fit an ensemble model output statistics (EMOS) distribution using censored regression: the predictive location and spread depend on ensemble forecasts. Their application uses 48-hour maximum wind forecasts from the University of Washington mesoscale ensemble and observed wind, comparing with the raw ensemble and climatology. This is probabilistic calibration, not a deterministic 100 m IFS/ERA5 correction. It is a useful next step only when ensemble members and appropriate reference observations are available; scores such as CRPS and calibration complement deterministic MAE. A separate peer-reviewed [comparison of 100 m wind ensemble post-processing at onshore and offshore sites](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/apme/53/4/jamc-d-13-0162.1.xml) is relevant to the target height, but is not presented here as a verified implementation for this repository.

**ECMWF solar ensemble repository.** The authors' [probabilistic solar forecasting repository](https://github.com/wentingwang94/probabilistic-solar-forecasting) describes ECMWF ENS forecasts for seven US SURFRAD stations and the Jacumba solar plant for 2017–2020, plus forecast and power-related files. It provides a station solar post-processing script and separate gradient-boosting and physical model-chain irradiance-to-power scripts. These are distinct tasks: power conversion is not evidence that a model corrects solar irradiance. The README alone does not establish the exact run issue times, forecast provenance, train/test separation, spatial selection or interval alignment. Inspect the data and scripts and verify those details before treating the dataset as an as-issued day-ahead example; these US station and plant data are not UK site validation.

## Forecast evaluation is a separate task

[WeatherBench 2](https://github.com/google-research/weatherbench2) is an evaluation framework and forecast data collection, not itself a fitted correction to IFS. Its [peer-reviewed paper](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2023MS004019) discusses evaluation against ERA5 and operational analyses, and the [submission documentation](https://weatherbench2.readthedocs.io/en/latest/submit.html) specifies conservative regridding and notes that scores at different resolutions need not be comparable. The [FAQ](https://sites.research.google/gr/weatherbench/faq) says operational IFS configuration changes over time and distinguishes real-time analysis from retrospective ERA5. Use it for evaluation design, not as proof that a correction trained on ERA5 improves site forecasts. Its global benchmark resolutions and variables must be checked against this project's hourly 100 m wind and solar needs.

The [WeatherBench 2 data guide](https://weatherbench2.readthedocs.io/en/latest/data-guide.html) lists IFS HRES forecasts from 2016–2022 at 00/12 UTC, including a 0.25° product and corresponding ERA5 datasets in a public cloud bucket. On 6 November 2026 we successfully read the public metadata of `2016-2022-0012-1440x721.zarr` and 16 bytes of one wind data chunk without authentication: its variables include **10 m wind** but do not include **100 m wind or downward solar radiation**. That makes it a potential UK 10 m wind learning exercise, not a direct source for both of our intended targets. The guide instructs users to check the licence for each dataset; we have not verified the licence or decoded a sample field. Its documented time and lead coordinates should be preserved when pairing with ERA5.

ERA5 incorporates observations through an ECMWF model and data assimilation; ERA5 and IFS share institutional and modelling lineage. Therefore an IFS-versus-ERA5 score measures agreement with that gridded reference, not fully independent observational validation. A station can itself be subject to representativeness error and may have contributed to assimilation: document the observation source and use, and distinguish independence from the forecast verification data from complete independence of every underlying observation. See [ECMWF ERA5 documentation](https://confluence.ecmwf.int/pages/viewpage.action?pageId=242085805).

## Practical experiment implied by these examples

1. Preserve one identifiable IFS run per decision, its initialization, lead, actual availability, model cycle, raw values and retrieval metadata. Label reforecasts separately from operational as-issued forecasts. Pair each target with reference version and availability; a reanalysis produced later is a valid target only once available to the simulated training process.
2. Align location and physical quantity explicitly. Record selected grid coordinates, site distance and land/sea context; for gridded alternatives document interpolation and masks. Pair instantaneous wind with the same instant, and radiation energy or mean flux with the same interval and units. See [the detailed matching note](forecast-reference-matching.md) for the project's UTC and accumulation rules.
3. Fit corrections on past forecast–reference pairs, with chronological day-based splits and an untouched later period. Compare raw IFS, training-period mean-bias correction and a regularized linear model against any more complex model on identical rows. Evaluate wind and daylight solar separately using bias, MAE and RMSE, skill relative to raw IFS, sample counts, and slices by lead, season and site type. Use day blocks for uncertainty and hold out sites separately when the intended use includes unseen locations.
4. Add station, buoy or site measurements when feasible. Improvements against ERA5 alone remain improvements against ERA5; neither that outcome nor a single UK case establishes improvements against measured farm weather or generation.
